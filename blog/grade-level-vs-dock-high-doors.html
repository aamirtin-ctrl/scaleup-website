Grade-level vs dock-high doors: which do you need?

By the ScaleUp principals — 90+ combined years in construction, industrial operations & finance Published July 7, 2026 4 min read

Most small-bay flex tenants want grade-level doors, not dock-high. A grade-level door sits at the parking lot, so anything on wheels drives or rolls straight in. A dock-high door sits about four feet up, built for backing a semi-trailer against the building so the trailer floor lines up with your warehouse floor. If you're not moving full 53-foot trailers on a regular schedule, grade-level is the right answer, and it's what most flex parks are built around.

Here's the situation that decides it. You've leased a bay, a delivery shows up, and either the truck pulls in and unloads at floor level, or the driver stands on the ground handing boxes up over a four-foot lip. Which one you want depends entirely on the trucks that actually come to your door.

What grade-level means

A grade-level door is a roll-up door at ground height. The lot, the door threshold, and your slab all sit at roughly the same elevation, so a box truck, a sprinter van, a flatbed, a forklift, or a pallet jack crosses the threshold without a ramp. In a 1,500 to 3,000 SF flex unit this is the standard, and it's why a contractor can drive a work truck inside at night or a distributor can wheel pallets straight off a 24-foot box truck.

Door size matters within grade-level. A 12x12 door clears a box truck and most work vehicles; a 14-foot-tall door clears a tall cube van or a small dump truck with the bed raised. Walk the approach before you sign, because a door only helps if your truck can line up with it. That's item four on our flex space lease checklist, and it trips up more tenants than door height does.

What dock-high means

A dock-high door sits about 48 inches above the drive, so a tractor-trailer backs in and the trailer floor meets your warehouse floor. A forklift then rolls straight from the building into the trailer. This is how big-box distribution warehouses move freight, and if you receive or ship full trailers several times a week, it saves real labor.

The cost is flexibility. A dock-high door with no truck backed against it is a four-foot drop you can't drive anything into. Big-box warehouse and small-bay flex are built for different jobs, which we lay out in flex space vs. traditional warehouse. Most small businesses ship LTL (less-than-truckload) on a box truck or a common carrier, and a box truck doesn't need a dock.

How to decide

Look at the largest vehicle that will regularly come to your unit.

  • Vans, box trucks, flatbeds, your own work vehicles: grade-level.
  • Full 48 or 53-foot trailers, multiple times a week: dock-high, and you probably want a bigger footprint than a flex bay anyway.
  • An occasional trailer delivery: grade-level plus a dock plate or a portable yard ramp handles it.

Most flex tenants land in the first bucket. If a full trailer shows up once a quarter with your annual inventory, the carrier can usually break it onto a box truck for local delivery, or you rent a yard ramp for the day.

Can you add a dock later

Sometimes, but treat it as construction, not a tweak. Cutting a dock into an existing slab means excavating a depressed pit or building an exterior ramp, adding a leveler and bumpers, and confirming the truck court has enough depth for a trailer to swing in. That last part is the real limiter in a small-bay park, where drive aisles are sized for vans and box trucks, not for a semi to jackknife into a dock. If dock-high is genuinely part of your operation, lease a building designed for it from day one rather than paying to retrofit one that wasn't.

Before you tour, count your trucks: write down the largest and most frequent vehicle that will come to your door, and let the door type follow from that instead of the other way around. FAQ

What is the difference between grade-level and dock-high doors?

A grade-level door is at ground height, so anything on wheels drives straight in. A dock-high door sits about four feet up so a semi-trailer's floor lines up with the warehouse floor. Grade-level suits vans and box trucks; dock-high suits full trailers.

Do I need a dock-high door for a small business?

Usually not. If your deliveries come on box trucks, vans, or common carriers, a grade-level door is faster and more flexible. Dock-high only pays off when you load full 48 or 53-foot trailers on a regular schedule.

Can you convert a grade-level door to dock-high?

It is possible but it is construction, not a small change: you excavate a pit or build a ramp, add a leveler, and confirm the truck court is deep enough for a trailer to back in. In most small-bay parks it is cheaper to lease a dock-designed building than to retrofit one.
