Shop Space for Contractors: What a Trades Business Actually Needs

By the ScaleUp principals — 90+ combined years in construction, industrial operations & finance Published August 8, 2026 6 min read

A trades business needs three things from a shop, and square footage is not one of them: a door your truck fits through, power your tools actually run on, and room to stage tomorrow's work without unloading it twice. Get those right and the footage sorts itself out.

Most of the crews we lease to came from a driveway, a rented yard, or a self-storage unit they were quietly working out of. Here is what they wish they had checked first.

The door, and the approach to it

A 12x12 grade-level door takes a box truck. A 10x10 takes a van and makes a box truck a problem. If your largest vehicle lives inside overnight, measure it with the ladder rack on, because that is the number that matters.

Then walk the approach before you sign. A door you cannot line up on is a door you cannot use. Parking rows, islands, and neighbouring tenants' trailers all decide whether a truck can swing in straight or has to be backed in blind from the drive aisle. We have watched tenants at other parks give up on a bay for exactly this reason.

Dock-high is usually the wrong answer for trades. You want to drive in, not lift up. The full comparison is in grade-level vs dock-high doors.

Power, checked at the panel

Ask what service the unit has and then go look at the panel. Brochures say "ample power" and mean nothing by it. Welders, compressors, lifts, and most fabrication equipment want 3-phase, and adding 3-phase to a building that does not have it runs into five figures and a utility timeline you do not control.

If you are running hand tools and a small compressor, single-phase is fine and you should not pay for what you will not use. If you are not sure which side of that line you are on, how much power a shop actually needs walks through it by trade.

Staging space is not storage space

Storage is inventory that sits. Staging is tomorrow's job, laid out in the order it gets loaded. They are different square footage and crews consistently under-budget the second one.

A practical rule: if two people load the truck at 6am, they need floor they can walk around the material, not floor the material is stacked on. Count the footprint of a full day's material laid out flat, then add the vehicle if it parks inside. That number, plus racking along the walls, is your bay.

The front of the unit

Every trades tenant says they do not need an office and about half of them build one in the first year. You need somewhere to put a desk, a printer, and a person answering the phone, and somewhere a customer or an inspector can sit down. An air-conditioned front already finished into the unit costs you nothing extra at signing and saves a buildout later.

A restroom in your own bay matters more than it sounds. Shared facilities down the row become a daily friction for a crew that starts before the park opens.

How much space to take

Two to four people with one truck loading daily fit comfortably in 1,500 SF. Four to eight people, or two vehicles staging, want 2,000 SF. If customers come to you and need to see product, the 3,000 SF glass-front units are a different conversation. The sizing math is in what size flex space your business needs.

Take the bigger bay if you are between two sizes and growing. Stepping up one size costs a few hundred dollars a month. Moving a shop costs you weeks of production, and small-business formation across Texas has stayed strong enough that the bay you leave gets taken (Dallas Fed indicators).

FAQ

What size shop space does a contractor need?

Two to four people running one truck fit comfortably in about 1,500 SF. Four to eight people, or two vehicles staging at once, want closer to 2,000 SF. Size around the footprint of a full day's material laid out flat, plus any vehicle that parks inside overnight, plus racking along the walls.

Do I need 3-phase power for a contractor shop?

You need it if you run welders, compressors, lifts, or most fabrication equipment. Hand tools and a small compressor run fine on single-phase. Check the panel rather than the brochure, because adding 3-phase to a building that does not have it runs into five figures and a utility timeline you do not control.

Is grade-level or dock-high better for a trades business?

Grade-level, in almost every case. Trades load vehicles, not freight trailers, so you want to drive in at floor level rather than lift up to a dock. A 12x12 grade-level door takes a box truck; a 10x10 takes a van and makes a box truck difficult.
